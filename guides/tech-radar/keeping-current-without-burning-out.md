# Keeping current without burning out

**TL;DR:** The infosphere generates more "must read" content per day than you
could read in a year. The trick to staying current isn't reading more; it's
designing an information diet that prioritizes durable signal over novelty
noise. The discipline: small set of high-quality sources, batched consumption,
the "wait and see" filter on hype, and explicit permission to ignore most of
what's loud.

## The problem

The default mode for tech-curious people:

- HN open in a tab, refreshed during compile times.
- Twitter / X / Bluesky / Mastodon timelines.
- Slack channels for every interesting community.
- YouTube subscriptions to a dozen technical channels.
- Daily newsletters.
- Weekly newsletters.
- The pile of "I'll read this later" bookmarks.

The cumulative time spent is large. The signal extracted is small. The
mental tax of "I'm behind" is constant. The actual work suffers because
context-switching costs more than the input is worth.

The solution is not "read faster"; it's "read less, on purpose."

## The diet hierarchy

In rough order of durable signal per minute spent:

1. **Books** — high latency to publication, high curation, deep content.
2. **Long-form articles + papers** — slower than blog posts; better thought
   out.
3. **Conference talks** (recorded) — 30-45 min of distilled expertise.
4. **Curated newsletters** — someone else's filter on the firehose.
5. **Quality blog posts** (curated authors, not random links) — selective
   reading.
6. **Documentation + changelogs** for tools you actually use.
7. **Podcasts** — passive learning during commutes / chores.
8. **Aggregator front pages** (HN, Lobsters) — wide coverage, low signal.
9. **Social media** — high noise, occasional gems, addictive.

Spend more time at the top of this list, less at the bottom.

## The "one signal per week" rule

Per source, ask: how many *actionable* signals did this give me last quarter?

- A newsletter with 1-2 actionable items per month: keep.
- A newsletter with 0 actionable items in 3 months: unsubscribe.
- A Twitter feed with 1 actionable thread per month: keep.
- A Twitter feed with 0 in 3 months: unfollow.

This is brutally honest auditing. Most sources fail this test. Cutting them
*does not* leave you ignorant; it leaves you with more time for the sources
that pass.

## Batching — the cheapest improvement

Random-access consumption (HN every coffee break) costs more than scheduled
consumption (HN once on Friday morning).

- **News aggregators**: once a week, time-boxed (30 min).
- **Social media**: once a day, time-boxed (15 min).
- **YouTube / talks**: batched weekly during a planned session.
- **Newsletters**: collected during the week, read on Saturday.
- **Conference talks**: queue them; watch one per Sunday afternoon.

The shift from "I might be missing something" to "I'll catch it Friday"
gives back significant focused time during the week.

## The "wait for the second post" filter

The pattern in any new technology / framework / paradigm:

1. **Announcement post.** By the creator. Beautifully marketed.
2. **HN front page.** Lots of "this looks cool" comments.
3. **First "deep dive" by someone unrelated.** Six weeks later.
4. **First production case study.** Six months later.
5. **Counter-thought piece** ("X considered harmful"). A year later.

Reading 1-2 is fun but informationally useless. Wait for 3-4 before
investing real time. Read 5 before committing to or against the technology.

This costs you nothing — the technology isn't going anywhere. It saves you
the cost of evaluating things that don't pan out (most things don't).

## Distinguishing hype from durable shifts

Markers of hype that won't last:

- **Vendor / VC funding driving most of the coverage.**
- **No production case studies beyond the creator's company.**
- **A "killer feature" that requires significant infrastructure to use.**
- **Marketing that focuses on "what" not "why".**
- **The same paragraph reposted across blogs.**

Markers of a durable shift:

- **Multiple independent implementations.** Standards bodies forming.
- **Production case studies from companies with skin in the game.**
- **Stories of *migration to* it from previous tech, with regret about
  delaying the migration.**
- **Coverage from skeptical authors who got convinced.**
- **A second-mover ecosystem** — books, courses, conferences.

Most "new" things don't survive the 2-year test. The handful that do became
load-bearing. The 2-year delay in adopting them costs little compared to
the time saved by ignoring the casualties.

## The minimum viable input stack

A baseline that works for a working engineer:

- **1 book on the go** — read 30-60 min daily during commutes or before bed.
- **2-3 high-signal newsletters** — read weekly. Examples: Console, Pointer,
  KubeWeekly, Bytecode Alliance, language-specific (Go Weekly, Python Weekly,
  This Week in Rust).
- **5-10 RSS feeds** — primary sources only (e.g., your top tools' blogs,
  a few high-quality individual blogs). Read in batch sessions.
- **1 conference per year** — attend; consume the recordings of the others.
- **1 hour per week** of "deep dive" — explore something on the Trial ring
  of your radar.

That's it. Everything else is optional.

## Newsletter triage

A specific protocol for newsletter overload:

- **Subscribe to anything you're curious about.**
- **Set a folder rule** that filters newsletters into a "newsletters" folder.
- **Don't read them as they arrive.**
- **One day a week**, open the folder. Skim each newsletter:
  - Anything useful? Read it.
  - Generic content? Mark as read, move on.
- **At quarter-end**, audit which newsletters delivered actionable content.
  Unsubscribe from the rest.

This treats newsletters like a feed (your control) instead of like email
(their control).

## "I'll read this later" — the bookmark graveyard

The pattern: you find an interesting article. You can't read it now. You
bookmark it. Repeat 50 times. You never read any of them.

Counter-tactics:

- **Time-box reading sessions** specifically for the bookmark queue
  (Sunday afternoon for 30 min).
- **Capture the *insight*, not the URL**, when you can. Three sentences of
  notes ages better than a bookmark.
- **Accept that most bookmarks will go unread.** Periodically clear the
  queue. The articles you *really* needed will come back via other channels.
- **Read-later services** (Pocket, Instapaper, Readwise) help only if you
  use them. If they become bookmark v2, kill them.

## The conference cadence

Annual conferences are deceptively expensive (travel, time off, $$, energy).
The compensating tactic:

- **Attend in person once a year**, max. Choose carefully.
- **Watch the recordings of 3-5 other conferences per year.** Free (mostly),
  asynchronous, no jet lag.
- **Pick talks by speakers, not by topic titles.** Speakers earn trust over
  time.

A talk from a speaker you trust on a topic you don't care about is often
more valuable than a talk from an unknown on a topic you care about.

## The "what's changed in the last 5 years?" check

Periodically (yearly), ask: what's *materially different* about how I work
now vs 5 years ago?

If the answer is "nothing", your information diet is broken — you're consuming
but not converting.

If the answer is "I switched everything" — your information diet is too
turbulent. You're chasing fashion.

The healthy answer is "2-3 substantial shifts" per 5 years. Maybe a new
language, a new platform, a new technique. Anchored by a lot of continuity.

## Specific habits that compound

- **Write what you learn.** Notes, blog posts, internal wiki. The act of
  writing forces you to actually understand it. Months later, your notes
  are the search-index for "wait, what was that thing I learned?".
- **Teach what you learn.** Lunch-and-learns, conference talks, mentoring.
  The fastest way to consolidate.
- **Build a small thing per quarter** with a Trial-ring technology. The
  weekend project that doesn't have to ship.
- **Re-read foundational books.** "Designing Data-Intensive Applications" or
  "The Pragmatic Programmer" benefit from re-reading every few years.

## When the "stay current" anxiety is the actual problem

Sometimes the issue isn't the firehose; it's the underlying anxiety
("am I falling behind?"). Counter-tactics:

- **Notice the anxiety.** Naming it weakens its grip.
- **Audit what "behind" means.** Behind on what? At what job? For whose
  benefit?
- **Compare yourself to yourself, not to Twitter.** Are you better at your
  craft than 6 months ago?
- **Lean into depth over breadth.** Deep understanding of one stack
  outcompetes shallow familiarity with ten.

The engineers who get the best work done aren't the ones who follow every
trend. They're the ones who pick a stack, go deep, and adopt new tech when
it solves a problem they actually have.

## Gotchas

- **Substack-bait taglines** trigger curiosity faster than they deliver
  insight. Develop immunity.
- **AI-generated content blooms** make casual aggregator pages worse over
  time. Curated sources matter more, not less.
- **Algorithmic feeds** (YouTube recommendations, Twitter "For You") are
  optimized for engagement, not signal. Treat the algorithm as adversarial.
- **FOMO peaks at conferences and product launches.** Plan ahead: those
  moments are when discipline matters most.
- **Information overload is a status symbol in some circles.** Resist the
  pressure to perform busyness with information.

## When you should consume more, not less

- **Starting a new role or domain.** The first 3 months in a new area is
  the rare time to flood-feed.
- **Active major project decisions.** Choosing a database / cloud / framework
  for a multi-year commitment warrants deep evaluation.
- **You've coasted for a while and feel stagnant.** A deliberate "explore
  phase" can be healthy. Time-box it.

But default mode is **less, on purpose.**
